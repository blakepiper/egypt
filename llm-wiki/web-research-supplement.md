# Web research supplement

This page records the limited current-source check used in the second pass. It is not a substitute for the archive; it verifies chronology, corrects student answers, and updates one research question. Links were checked 2026-08-30.

## Funerary corpora overlap rather than replace one another

UCL's Digital Egypt overview defines the modern corpus names by their principal surviving media: Pyramid Texts on Old Kingdom pyramid interiors, Coffin Texts mainly on Middle Kingdom coffins and burial chambers, and Book of the Dead compositions especially on later papyrus rolls. Its Coffin Text page notes derivation from Pyramid Texts and reuse in the Book of the Dead. This independently supports the course's overlap or “roof shingles” model.

UCL also emphasizes that “Book of the Dead” is a modern name for a repertoire of roughly 175–200 compositions rather than one fixed scripture. Early “going out by day” formulae appear on a Seventeenth Dynasty queen's coffin; papyrus placement becomes regular but not obligatory in the Eighteenth Dynasty; and chapter numbering is modern editorial work. The fact that compositions occur on papyrus, coffins, shrouds, walls, amulets, shabtis, heart scarabs, and other objects corrects the exam shorthand that makes the corpus only a papyrus book.

## Hunefer and the judgment scene

The British Museum's record for Hunefer's Nineteenth Dynasty papyrus identifies the figures and sequence: Hunefer adores deities, Anubis leads him to and adjusts the balance, the heart is weighed against Maat's feather, Ammit waits, Thoth records, Horus conducts Hunefer to Osiris, and Isis and Nephthys stand behind the throne. It also identifies the four Sons of Horus on a water lily beneath the throne and says the accompanying text includes Book of the Dead Spell 30B.

This corrects two common simplifications. Judgment is a designed sequence with multiple divine offices, not merely “heart versus feather.” And the water lily in this scene is not, by itself, evidence of intoxication: its placement under Osiris and the Sons of Horus participates in renewal and emergence.

- [British Museum: Book of the Dead of Hunefer, frame 3](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA9901-3)
- [British Museum: What is a Book of the Dead?](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/what-book-dead)
- [British Museum: heart scarab with Spell 30B](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA7876)

## Temples and divine manifestation

The Metropolitan Museum's current Temple of Dendur essay confirms the practical structure emphasized by Teeter: temples were divine residences; deities temporarily manifested in cult statues; the king was ideal officiant but priests served in practice; food, drink, incense, and purification formed daily cult; inner rooms were restricted; and festival processions brought the cult image into greater public proximity. It also explains the temple as an image of the created natural world.

- [Metropolitan Museum: The Temple's Cult and Decoration](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/temple-of-dendur-cult-and-decoration)

## Set remains double-sided

The British Museum's overview describes Seth as god of chaos, desert, storms, violence, and foreigners but also as Re's defender. Its collection contains a late Nineteenth Dynasty stela in which a Deir el-Medina official worships Seth, plus a Ramesside amulet of Seth wearing the double crown. These objects are a useful material corrective to the idea that the Osiris story made Set universally untouchable.

The collection's one-line biography (“god of chaotic forces; brother and slayer of Osiris”) is accurate but narrower than the combined evidence. Museum object records, the Contendings, Te Velde, and Blake's project together show worship, royal legitimacy, dangerous force, and solar protection alongside murder and disorder.

- [British Museum: Seth collection biography](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIOG55825)
- [British Museum: stela of Aapehty worshipping Seth](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA35630)
- [British Museum: Ramesside Seth amulet](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA22897)

## Amduat as dangerous travel and rebirth

The Metropolitan Museum's Amduat papyrus of Djedmutesankh preserves the eleventh and twelfth hours. Its catalog description identifies travel with the sun through a hazardous Duat, the mummified Osiris, Shu, the dawn scarab, and the moment before sunrise. The object also shows that Underworld Book imagery moved beyond royal tomb walls: this Third Intermediate Period papyrus was placed within a woman's mummy.

- [Metropolitan Museum: Amduat Papyrus of Djedmutesankh](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/550898)

## Blue water lily: a real update, with strict limits

A 2024 *Scientific Reports* study analyzed residues in a second-century BCE Bes-shaped vessel using proteomics, metabolomics, genetics, and infrared methods. The authors report Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea, Peganum harmala, a Cleome species, fermented fruit liquid, honey or royal-jelly-like ingredients, and human fluids. This is direct material evidence for blue water lily in a complex, potentially psychoactive Ptolemaic ritual mixture.

It changes the evidentiary picture since Blake wrote in 2017, but only within its scope:

- **Supported:** at least one late Egyptian ritual vessel held a deliberately compounded mixture containing blue water lily and other bioactive ingredients.
- **Not demonstrated:** a universal or millennia-long sacrament; the dose and subjective experience; that New Kingdom banquet flowers were consumed; that iconographic proximity to mandrake or poppy documents one recipe; or that solar symbolism secretly encodes pharmacology.

The 2004 pharmacology article in Blake's bibliography is much weaker archaeological evidence. It reasons from plant chemistry and images to ancient sexual use; it does not report residue from an Egyptian context. Modern commercial “blue lotus” products containing apomorphine/nuciferine also do not prove ancient preparation. The 2024 vessel study is stronger because it begins with an excavated-period object and surviving residue, though it remains a single late case.

- [Scientific Reports: Multianalytical investigation of a Ptolemaic Bes vase](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-78721-8)
- [PubMed record for the Bes-vase study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39537764/)
- [PubMed: Nymphaea cults in ancient Egypt and the New World](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14749409/)

## How to use web material here

Institutional collection records are strongest for the specific object they document. A museum's school page can offer a clear overview but should not override specialist argument. One residue analysis can establish one vessel's contents, not a civilization-wide norm. The web supplement therefore tightens scope; it does not manufacture certainty where the 2017 archive lacked it.
