# How this archive is built

The Living Archive is a guide to ancient Egyptian religion assembled from ancient texts in translation, modern scholarship, visual material, and interpretive notes. It treats each source according to what it can show and keeps uncertainty beside the claim it qualifies.

## A connected way to read

Ancient Egyptian religion changes across three thousand years, but several questions recur. How do gods and humans maintain life and order? How do images, names, speech, and offerings act in the world? How do temples, households, kingship, and tombs connect? What changes when a practice moves into a new place, medium, or period?

The reading routes begin with these questions and then follow them through creation traditions, local theology, kingship, ritual practice, personhood, funerary literature, and later transformations. Start with [[start-here]], then choose a route from [[course-reading-guide]] or the [[four-week-relearning-plan]].

## What the sources can support

The archive separates four kinds of material:

- **Primary source:** a translated ancient text, image, or object.
- **Scholarship:** a modern scholarly account or museum record.
- **Archive synthesis:** an interpretation assembled from the source collection. It is not scholarly consensus.
- **Contested:** a modern proposal whose evidence or method remains disputed.

This distinction matters throughout the site. A temple relief can show how a king was represented without proving how every person understood kingship. A funerary text can describe a desired afterlife without proving that every burial followed the same script. A modern theory can raise a worthwhile question without becoming evidence for ancient practice.

## Limits and gaps

Some source files are scans with little machine-readable text, and some translations are old. The Goelet and Faulkner edition of the *Book of the Dead* is not in the source collection, so the Plate 30 discussion draws on the surviving images, report, and related scholarship rather than pretending to reproduce missing pages.
