# Student-work reconstruction

This page preserves what Blake actually wrote in 2017 without confusing it with either Dr. BeDuhn's position or present scholarly consensus. It consolidates every substantive student-authored artifact, including draft-only material and mistakes corrected in later saves.

## Exam 1: myth, geography, history, and deities

Two completed Exam 1 files survive. They are different saves. The version inside the Exam 1 folder is fuller; the Drive export is an earlier or less complete state. Most shared answers are equivalent, but the fuller file adds material and changes the Wadjet identification from an incorrect match to **D, the cobra**, the standard identification.

Blake's exam reconstruction emphasizes these ideas:

- Egyptian myth could be **operative** (recitation effects what it describes), **etiological** (explains an origin), or a **charter** for institutions and social order.
- Egyptian gods were locally rooted and could be combined or identified without requiring one exclusive theology. The answer uses “henotheism” for devotion to one deity as supreme without denying others.
- **Kemet**, the Black Land, is fertile Nile soil; **Deshret**, the Red Land, is desert. Their contrast is ecological and symbolic, but desert is not simply metaphysical evil.
- **Maat** is truth/order/right relationship, while **isfet** is disruptive disorder. The king maintains maat in society, landscape, cult, and war.
- Hieroglyphs and images were potentially effective presences. This underlies mutilated dangerous signs, offerings in image, divine statues, and written names.
- In the Re creation material, Khepri names self-becoming, Shu and Tefnut emerge from the creator, and humans come from the divine Eye's tears through wordplay.
- Osiris's death, Isis's protective search and conception, Horus's succession, and Set's challenge supply a grammar for kingship—not one fixed canonical story.
- Aten becomes radically central under Amenhotep IV/Akhenaten, but solar Aten traditions predate him.

The matching sections attempted a wide deity field: Re, Amun, Atum, Khepri, Ptah, Khnum, Thoth, Khonsu, Osiris, Isis, Horus, Set, Hathor, Sekhmet, Neith, Sobek, Anubis, Taweret, Bes, Wadjet, Nekhbet, and others. Some entries were blank or uncertain. Those gaps are restored in [[deity-field-guide]], not guessed from the unfinished answer.

## Exam 2: institutions, personhood, ritual, and early funerary texts

### Terms Blake defined

- **heka:** divine/ritual power conventionally translated “magic.”
- **historiola:** a compact mythic precedent used to empower a present operation.
- **ib/ab:** heart as thought, memory, character, and witness.
- **ka:** life force or sustaining double, able to receive offerings.
- **ba:** mobile manifestation, often bird-bodied in art.
- **akh:** effective or transfigured dead person.
- **khaibit:** shadow as an aspect of embodied identity.
- **wab:** “pure” priest.
- **rekhet:** a female knower/medium in Teeter's discussion of personal religious consultation.
- **bak:** temple servant or devotee in the exam's usage.
- **bau:** a deity's manifestations or power; Blake's short gloss “bad omen” is too narrow and is corrected in [[glossary]].
- **shabti:** figurine answering for required labor in the afterlife.
- **natron:** naturally occurring evaporite salts used in purification and mummification; “sodium carbonate” is an incomplete shorthand.
- **tekenu:** enigmatic wrapped or bundled figure/object in funeral processions, not confidently reducible to discarded organs.
- **pylon:** monumental temple gateway.

### Social and temple system

Blake reads Ptahhotep as a hierarchical ethics of role fulfillment: officials owe hearing, justice, restraint, and generosity; subordinates are expected to work faithfully. The corrected wiki keeps this as an elite prescriptive ideal, not proof that Egyptian hierarchy was benign. The exam also stresses that the king is the theoretical chief officiant while priests perform daily service as his local proxies.

Priestly work includes rotating service, bodily and dietary purity, washing, abstention for a ritual interval, and specialized offices. The **hem-netjer** (“servant of the god”) is a high priestly designation; lector priests carry/read ritual books; sem-priests are recognizable by leopard skin and perform funerary ritual. Egypt did not have a single celibate, cloistered priesthood analogous to later monastic institutions.

The cult image receives morning awakening/opening, purification, clothing, anointing, food, drink, incense, praise, and the closing/sealing of the shrine. Food could be presented three times daily in some temples and then reverted to human use. The statue is an interface for divine manifestation, not merely decorative sculpture.

### Festivals and communication

The exam connects festivals to processional barks, public proximity to a normally restricted god, and oracle questions answered through the bark's movement. Blake suggested an **ideomotor** physical explanation for the bearers' motion. That is a modern hypothesis, not the ancient account and not demonstrable from the archive.

Blake associates the **Sed festival** with royal renewal: miniature shrines, offerings, boundary-running, and the king's tomb/afterlife identity. He describes **Khoiak** as Osirian renewal and mentions destructive treatment of Sethian figures or cakes. The broad association with Osiris, planting, and regeneration is sound; a single standardized “battle reenactment” should not be projected onto all Khoiak rites.

Dreams could be ordinary divine communication or intentionally sought through incubation. Letters to the dead enlisted deceased relatives in disputes and problems. Names were identity-bearing and could be invoked, preserved, attacked, or erased.

### Funeral and personhood

The exam distinguishes the accessible tomb chapel from the sealed burial chamber, notes mummification and canopic treatment, and connects the **Opening of the Mouth** to restored senses and agency. Anubis's embrace, mourning, processions, offerings, and the body's safe installation form a sequence rather than one isolated ceremony.

The heart, body, ka, ba, akh, shadow, and name are complementary aspects, not seven detachable “souls.” The nightly reunion of mobile ba and preserved body is a better model than escape from the body.

### Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, and Underworld Books

The exam preserves an interpretive debate. Conventional funerary reading treats the Pyramid Texts as mortuary compositions for the dead king. Naydler proposes that at least some utterances also encode living royal initiation, voluntary ritual death, or Sed-festival transformation. Blake saw evidence on both sides. [[pyramid-texts]] keeps the funerary context primary while treating ritual reuse and performativity as real possibilities short of Naydler's full thesis.

Blake's memorable Pyramid Text details include the king “departed alive,” ascent, divine integration, restored body and senses, food, a ladder/ferry, and the Cannibal Hymn's consumption of gods and their power. Coffin Text details include preventing decapitation, restoring mouth and anus, retaining the heart, becoming Khepri, refusing inverted excrement/urine, securing food and movement, and assigning labor to shabtis. These earthy anxieties matter: Egyptian afterlife texts are concerned with continued embodied agency, not only an abstract judgment.

The Amduat and Book of Gates organize the night into twelve divisions. Blake identifies Re's nocturnal ram-headed form, Khepri at rebirth, the bark, gates and guardians, regions of provision and punishment, Osiris, and Apep. The two books overlap without being identical: the Book of Gates foregrounds guarded thresholds; the Amduat more continuously maps transformations and populations through the hours.

## Exam 3: the Book of the Dead and late traditions

Blake correctly places the Book of the Dead from the New Kingdom through very late use, while recognizing material inherited from Coffin Texts. It is a repertoire from which manuscripts select, not one fixed canonical “book.” Papyrus rolls, mummy wrappings, coffins, sarcophagi, tomb walls, amulets, and funerary objects can bear its spells. **Rubrics** are red instructions or headings. A **vignette** is an accompanying image; Blake's answer wrongly defined it like a short myth, which is instead closer to a historiola.

The answers reconstruct a practical afterlife toolkit:

- bread and beer for provision;
- heart spells to prevent the heart from opposing its owner;
- ba and akh forms;
- transformations into birds, plants, snakes, and gods;
- knowledge of names for gates, portals, guardians, and places;
- sailing and “going out by day” as mobility;
- shabtis for labor;
- the four Sons of Horus and protected organs;
- solar praise, Osirian identification, Sokar–Osiris, and the Field of Reeds;
- the negative confession and heart weighing in the Hall of Two Truths.

The exam identifies Ani and his wife **Tutu** and notices her sistrum as a marker of temple-musical office. In the judgment scene Anubis handles the balance, Thoth records, Ammit threatens annihilation, and Osiris presides. The heart is weighed against Maat. Blake's interpretation of Taweret near the papyrus's end as rebirth imagery is plausible but should remain an interpretation, not a fixed caption supplied by the text.

The final answers gesture toward Egyptian religion's late reception in Greek and Demotic magic, Hermetic literature, Gnostic corpora such as Nag Hammadi, and Christianity. These connections require chronological precision. Survival, translation, polemic, adaptation, and visual borrowing are different processes; “Egypt influenced Christianity” is too broad to function as an explanation by itself.

## Set project: how the argument changed

The earliest drafts frame Set chiefly as Osiris's murderous, chaotic antagonist. Successive versions accumulate counterevidence: Set is an old god with cult centers and royal patrons; Horus and Set can be paired, separated, reconciled, or fused in royal imagery; Set's strength defends Re from Apep; foreignness can be politically useful as well as threatening; and demonization intensifies in particular later contexts. The final prose is therefore much less like a “devil biography” than the opening draft.

The finished report's major claims are:

1. Set's identity is historically variable. His strange animal is not securely identified as one zoological species.
2. Storm, desert, foreignness, violence, strength, confusion, and separation cluster around him, but no single English gloss exhausts him.
3. In the Horus conflict he can embody opposition necessary for the definition and reconciliation of kingship.
4. In the Osiris cycle he is murderer and sacrificial enemy, yet even hostile traditions can make him bear or restore what was damaged.
5. On Re's night bark he repels Apep; this makes Set and Apep categorically different.
6. Ramesside kings such as Seti I and II could honor him, demonstrating that negative myth did not erase cult.
7. Later Greek Typhon identifications and magical texts intensify monstrous and coercive associations.

The report uses Jungian language and suggests a **coniunctio oppositorum**, a union of opposites, in Horus–Set reconciliation. This is Blake's comparative interpretation, not an Egyptian technical doctrine. The drafts also contain unfinished placeholders for cult history and hieroglyphic meaning; their presence is part of the writing history, not evidence that the final project answered every question.

## Book of the Dead Plate 30 project

The assignment asked for a visual/textual report on a bounded portion of the Goelet/Faulkner papyrus of Ani. Blake's report identifies Plate 30 as a threshold before judgment, not the weighing itself. Ani approaches with Anubis, asserts purity and Osirian identity, and must know the names and structure of the place. Anubis's positive testimony functions as authorization to proceed.

The report's strongest insight is that knowledge is operative. Naming the floor, doorposts, bolt, gatekeeper, and divine actors does not merely pass a quiz; it demonstrates that Ani has become competent within sacred geography. “Osiris Ani” is likewise effective identification: the dead person enters Osiris's pattern of vindication and renewed life.

The supplied Plates 19, 29, 30, and the unified strip show that a papyrus roll is a designed sequence of text and image. The report should be read beside [[book-of-the-dead]], where its scene is distinguished from Chapter 125's better-known heart weighing.

## Blue water-lily project: complete argument and its limits

### Research question and thesis

The proposal asks whether the Egyptian blue water lily, **Nymphaea caerulea** (often called “blue lotus”), was only symbolic or also intentionally used for psychoactive, sedative, aphrodisiac, and possibly hallucinogenic effects. The final thesis advances from “possible use” to calling the plant a religious sacrament. That last formulation is the paper's conclusion, not a fact established by its sources.

### Evidence Blake assembled

- Botanical terminology is confused: true lotus (**Nelumbo**) and Egyptian water lilies (**Nymphaea**) are different plants. The Indian lotus arrived late; the native blue and white water lilies differ in color, leaf edge, and day/night flowering.
- Water-lily imagery is old. Early Dynastic vessels, rattles, models, later architecture, bouquets, banquets, funerals, music, perfume, and erotic scenes establish broad cultural importance.
- Opening above the water and association with Nefertem or the infant sun make the flower a powerful image of solar birth, beauty, scent, fertility, and renewal.
- Pyramid Text Utterance 249 identifies the king with Nefertem, the water lily at Re's nose. Book of the Dead Chapter 81 uses water-lily transformation. These securely support divine/solar and rebirth associations.
- The paper invokes Homer's Lotus-Eaters and proposes an Egyptian or North African setting. This is comparative speculation; Homer does not securely identify Nymphaea caerulea or document Egyptian ritual use.
- A Theban tomb scene, identified in the project as TT181, is read as combining water lily, mandrake, and poppy. The image establishes floral imagery more readily than species identification, dosing, or ingestion.
- Pharmacological sources associate commercial or botanical Nymphaea material with apomorphine/aporphine and nuciferine-related activity. The paper links this to sedation, arousal, dopamine effects, and altered consciousness.
- Blake proposes smelling flowers, steeping them in wine or liquid, or consuming preparations as delivery routes. The archive contains no Egyptian recipe demonstrating a dose or standardized ritual preparation.
- The project compares water-lily use in Maya and Egyptian imagery and surveys wider psychoactive-plant hypotheses. Similar images across cultures do not by themselves prove identical pharmacological practice.

### What the final paper itself admits

The conclusion explicitly concedes that no direct preparation or ingestion evidence had been found. It proposes future chemical work on vessels, residues, and mummies. That admission is crucial: the paper is an evidentiary argument under uncertainty, not a discovery report.

### What has changed since 2017

A 2024 multimethod residue study detected Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea together with Peganum harmala, Cleome, fermented fruit, honey/royal-jelly-like material, and human fluids in one second-century BCE Bes vessel. This materially strengthens the proposition that blue water lily could occur in a psychoactive ritual mixture in Ptolemaic Egypt. It still does not prove that New Kingdom banquet bouquets were drug delivery systems, that all water-lily symbolism was pharmacological, or that the plant functioned as a universal Egyptian “sacrament.” See [[web-research-supplement]].

## Source hierarchy for relearning

Use the student work as a map of what mattered to Blake and what the exams required. For historical claims, prefer in this order:

1. the ancient source in its date, genre, and material context;
2. current specialist scholarship or museum documentation;
3. Pinch, Teeter, Hornung, Sauneron, and Te Velde with attention to age and method;
4. Blake's reports and exam answers as 2017 learning evidence;
5. broad comparative or speculative papers only as hypotheses to test.
