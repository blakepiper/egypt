# Course-materials deep notes

These notes preserve the distinctive content of every surviving assigned packet. They supplement the thematic pages by keeping each reading's genre, sequence, and argumentative contribution visible.

## Pinch chapter 1 notes: the course's method

The instructor handout built the course around source criticism. Egyptian myth survives unevenly and is often embedded in hymns, ritual, magic, royal ideology, or funerary writing rather than collected as narrative scripture. Myth can explain origins, charter institutions, or operate ritually. Local cults generate multiple valid divine perspectives; national theology connects rather than necessarily abolishes them.

The notes tie ecology to symbolic geography: Kemet/Black Land is Nile fertility, Deshret/Red Land the desert beyond it, with the west strongly associated with burial and sunset. Maat and isfet express maintained order and its disruption. Hieroglyphic images are potentially effective, which helps explain substitution, mutilation of dangerous signs, and the importance of names.

The handout's periodization moves from royal Pyramid Texts to wider Coffin Texts and later Book of the Dead material. That can be a useful memory aid, but it should not be read as a clean replacement or a simple democratic march. Old compositions survive, new media and patrons emerge, and access remains unequal. This is the course's “roof shingles” model developed in [[funerary-text-tradition]].

## Creation packets

### Creations of Re

This packet is a collection rather than one creation story. Atum/Khepri describes self-generation before sky, earth, or stable place. Shu and Tefnut emerge from the creator's body; the returning Eye is replaced or enraged, and human beings arise from the Eye's tears through sound-play. The creator works through heart, naming, speech, bodily emissions, and transformation—several models at once.

The **Destruction of Mankind** shifts from first creation to crisis. Re discovers human rebellion; his Eye becomes Hathor/Sekhmet and slaughters. Red-dyed beer floods the land, the goddess drinks it as blood, becomes intoxicated, and humanity survives. Re withdraws upward on the cow Nut; Shu supports the sky; stars and celestial fields are organized. The tale explains both divine distance and ongoing ritual obligations. It also refuses a simple omnipotence model: divine government deliberates, becomes angry, changes strategy, and requires material ruse.

Other excerpts make Heka, Thoth, Sia, the divine word, and serpent forms part of creative and protective intelligence. Ritual instructions at the end show that cosmogony is not merely remembered: a practitioner can mobilize first-time events in a present operation.

### Cow Mother / Neith at Esna

The creator exists alone in Nun and self-emerges before sexual differentiation. The text calls her father of fathers and mother of mothers and identifies her principally with Neith while moving through cow and fish/lates imagery. Light comes from her eyes; dry land is established; thirty gods arise through their names; Re develops in an egg; humans come from tears and gods from saliva. Apep originates from spittle cast away, turning rejected bodily material into persistent threat.

This is a late local theology, not proof of one prehistoric universal “mother goddess.” Its importance lies in how Esna makes its own deity prior to the familiar national gods.

### Memphite Theology

The Shabaka Stone claims to copy a worm-damaged older text. That claim and the text's actual date/composition history are debated. Its first movement is political: Geb divides Egypt between Horus and Set, then gives the whole inheritance to Horus, with Memphis marking reunion. Its theological movement makes Ptah's heart and tongue prior to the Ennead. Perception reaches the heart; the tongue pronounces; names, bodies, crafts, towns, shrines, offerings, and moral distinctions take form.

The packet's commentary reviews interpretations of the text as dramatic ritual, philosophical treatise, archaizing political theology, or a combination. The safe conclusion is not “Egyptians invented Logos.” It is that this monument gives thought, authoritative utterance, craft, and political unification a Ptah-centered cosmic priority.

### Khonsu Cosmogony

This Ptolemaic inscription comes from a hidden room in the Khonsu temple at Karnak and is extraordinarily synthetic. It coordinates Amun-Re/Kematef, Ptah, the Hermopolitan Ogdoad, Khonsu, Hathor, Thoth, and Osiris. Thoth is distributed through faculties such as Hu (authoritative speech), Sia (perception/knowledge), Maa (seeing), and Sedem (hearing). Place-name and divine-name puns make Thebes and its gods the origin behind other centers.

The scholarly summary notes that Ptah is incorporated but subordinated to Theban priority and that Osiris can feel artificially inserted. This matters: syncretism is active priestly composition, not evidence that all Egyptian traditions had always been identical.

## Osirian readings

### Great Hymn to Osiris

The hymn presents Osiris through cult centers, epithets, universal kingship, fertility, burial, restoration, Isis's protection, Horus's conception, and the son's vindication. It is the fullest surviving Egyptian account in the packet, but it still avoids directly narrating Set's murder. The omission is part of the religious strategy: praise makes restored order present while dangerous events remain indirect.

Osiris is both local and expansive—lord of Busiris and Abydos, ruler of the dead, source of vegetation and inundation, and model for the deceased. Isis's agency is central: she searches, shelters, fends off disturbance, creates an heir, nurses him, and secures his recognition. Horus's enthronement completes succession; funerary offering connects the cosmic story to each beneficiary.

### Lamentations of Isis and Nephthys

This Ptolemaic composition is a performed rite appended to a Book of the Dead manuscript. Two women impersonate Isis and Nephthys, with shaved bodies, inscribed shoulders, water jars, and loaves. The text is to be recited in a secluded place at specified hours, including the third and eighth. A temple ritual for Osiris has been adapted to an individual burial.

The sisters summon Osiris to return, awaken, see them, receive embrace, and reclaim his house. Repetition is ritual labor, not literary redundancy. Osiris expands into sun, moon, Nile, grain, and kingship; Horus rules while the dead god remains potent in another mode. Grief, erotic intimacy, kinship, and cosmic renewal work together.

### Contendings of Horus and Seth

The Ramesside tale uses colloquial, episodic narration and can be comic, obscene, juridical, and mythic at once. The tribunal has deliberated for eighty years. Neith recommends Horus for the office while proposing compensation for Set, but Re resists and insults Horus's youth. Isis infiltrates proceedings, tricks Set into judging his own claim against him, and repeatedly drives the plot.

The rivals transform into hippopotami; Isis fashions a harpoon, first strikes Horus accidentally, then cannot kill her brother Set. Horus decapitates Isis; Hathor restores his eyes with gazelle milk. A sexual contest attempts to establish dominance, but Isis intercepts Set's semen and places Horus's on lettuce, after which Thoth summons it from Set's body. They race stone boats; Horus cheats with a wooden boat plastered to look like stone. Finally Osiris threatens the tribunal from the realm of the dead. Horus is installed, while Set joins Re and becomes thunder/storm and defender against Apep.

The ending prevents the easy equation Set = absolute evil. Succession is resolved, but dangerous strength is reassigned rather than erased.

## Ptahhotep

The frame presents an aged official requesting relief and permission to educate a successor. The king answers that wisdom is teachable because no one is born knowing. The maxims are practical, situational, and hierarchical rather than organized as abstract doctrine.

Their recurring counsel includes controlling speech in disputes with superiors, equals, and inferiors; allowing a petitioner to empty the heart; avoiding greed and predatory accumulation; treating guests and dependents generously; transmitting messages exactly; judging without favoritism; accepting the instability of wealth; cultivating a household and posterity; and knowing when silence is more effective than display. Maat is described as lasting, while wrongdoing may enrich someone only temporarily.

The text also contains social and gender assumptions that should not be idealized: class hierarchy is accepted; sexuality and women can be described from an elite male viewpoint; family control is valued. Yet a striking maxim says wisdom is not confined to elites and may be found even among women at the mill. “No one reaches the limit of skill” makes teachability a corrective to inherited status.

The ending returns to hearing: a good son hears and repeats; failure to hear is a moral and practical incapacity. Knowledge survives as an embodied chain of disciplined reception and speech.

## Hornung: maat and personhood

Hornung treats maat as the foundation or base upon which ordered life stands and as the food/air of gods and humans. Maat is Re's daughter and is offered back to him in temple ritual. Crucially, people must **do maat** and **speak maat**. It is active repair and appropriate measure, not passive conformity to a frozen system. Excess at either extreme can destroy proportion. Law, social ethics, cosmic cycles, art, and offering converge in this task. The restored Eye of Horus is a compact image of what has been fragmented being made whole and returned.

The personhood chapter begins from bodily survival. Mummification, canopic treatment, divine limb litanies, the tekenu, and rituals of restoration protect a body that remains necessary. The **ka** is life force, double, and recipient/vehicle of sustenance; the **ba** is a mobile manifestation that can leave the tomb and take forms; the **akh** is the effective/transfigured dead; the **heart** thinks, remembers, plans, and witnesses; **name** and **shadow** preserve other dimensions of identity. The ba's nightly return to the mummy is reunion, not liberation from a discarded shell.

## Pyramid Text selection

The packet moves through utterances for bodily awakening, offerings, ascent, entry among stars and gods, and control over hostile forces. The king is told that he has not departed dead but “departed alive.” Eyes, mouth, limbs, heart, and senses are restored. Horus and Set can each grasp or strengthen the king, showing that even oppositional gods contribute to a total royal body.

Utterance 249 makes the king Nefertem, the water lily at Re's nose. Other passages identify him with Sia, place him in solar boats, provide ladders and ferrymen, and require gates or beings to recognize his authority. Justice/right order appears alongside sheer potency.

The Cannibal Hymn, Utterances 273–274, imagines the king hunting, cooking, and eating gods, swallowing their magic, akhs, and knowledge. It is not a dietary report. It is an extreme ritual-poetic assertion that no divine power remains outside the king.

The material supports several registers: funerary provision for a dead king, liturgical performance, royal cosmic identity, and possible ritual reuse. Its archaeological placement in pyramid burial chambers is firm; a full living-initiation system is not directly documented.

## Naydler's alternative reading

Naydler argues that modern Egyptology too quickly reduced the Pyramid Texts to beliefs about a corpse. He interprets “voluntary death,” ascent, divine identification, the Sed festival, and architectural directionality as traces of an initiatory practice performed by a living king or substitute. The Duat becomes the inward source of renewal; the king becomes a shamanic mediator who dies and returns.

His reading of Unas maps text placement: south/west movement toward death, then east/north movement toward rebirth and emergence. He uses Sed-festival architecture and the Cannibal Hymn to reconstruct ecstatic incorporation of divine power.

The course value is the argument, not compulsory assent. Texts can be performative and buildings can stage transformation while still being made for burial. Naydler's thesis exceeds the surviving direct evidence when it turns those possibilities into a complete initiatory scenario.

## Coffin Text selection

The spells obsess over continued function. The dead person must not have the head cut off, must regain heart, mouth, anus, limbs, breath, memory, food, movement, and the ability to go out by day. Some reject eating feces or drinking urine—an anxiety about reversal and degradation. Others make the speaker Khepri, a bird, a god, or a being whose limbs are divine.

The Field of Offerings resembles ideal Egyptian agriculture, including work. Shabtis answer when labor is demanded. Ferries, gates, waterways, and hostile beings require knowledge and power. A heart spell asks that the heart not testify against its owner.

One selection reworks Cannibal Hymn material and shows textual transmission with corruption and adaptation. The corpus therefore preserves older royal language while extending mortuary resources to new patrons. “Democratization” is too simple: access broadens but remains material, regional, and status-dependent.

## Amduat and Book of Gates visual packets

Both packets present the solar night as twelve ordered divisions. Re enters the west aged or ram-headed, travels by bark through the Duat, energizes its dead and gods, overcomes Apep, and emerges with Khepri at dawn. Images and labels are a knowledge-map: knowing inhabitants, places, actions, and threats helps participate in the successful cycle.

The **Amduat** usually arranges a central solar-bark register between upper and lower populations. Mounds, caves, corpses, serpents, water, fire, deities, blessed dead, and punished enemies change from hour to hour. The middle night intensifies the Re–Osiris encounter; later hours bind or cut Apep, reverse death, and prepare birth. In the twelfth, beings move through a great serpent and emerge renewed.

The **Book of Gates** marks transitions with guarded gates and great serpents. Re's bark passes named keepers; Osiris and a judgment scene become prominent; restrained enemies and fiery regions show the fate of disorder. One celebrated register distinguishes human populations under solar care, complicating any claim that Egyptian cosmic order recognized only Egyptians. The final scene makes Nut, Nun, the solar bark, and emergence into a compact image of regenerated cosmos.

## Book of the Dead Plate 30 packet

The instructions required identification of the selected plate, summary of text and imagery, relation to surrounding plates, interpretation, and research support. The supplied images make Plate 30 part of a roll, not a self-contained poster. Blake's report correctly centers it on entrance and authorization before judgment. The ODT's appended water-lily prose is accidental contamination and belongs to the research-paper history.

## PDM/PGM and Plutarch: late transformations

The PDM/PGM selections are recipes and invocations from a multilingual late environment. They combine Egyptian gods, Greek divine names, voces magicae, materia, drawings, timing, and spoken commands. Seth/Typhon can appear in separation, attraction, coercion, revelation, and hostile operations, sometimes through donkey or dung associations and as the god who killed his brother. This is evidence for late transformation and ritual bricolage, not a timeless key to Old Kingdom Set.

Plutarch gives a continuous story: Osiris's and other gods' intercalary birthdays, Osiris as civilizer, Typhon's seventy-two conspirators and fitted chest, Isis's search, Anubis, the fourteen-part dismemberment, the missing phallus, and battles involving Horus. He repeatedly allegorizes Egyptian traditions through Greek philosophical categories. His account is invaluable precisely as a dated act of interpretation.
